We ran Queen 2.3.0 and Kafka 4.3.1 on the same three GCP brokers with local NVMe disks, with the
same load generators, the same load and the same report. Up to 10,000 partitions Kafka is the
faster of the two. Its median is three to six times lower with 256-byte messages and a little
lower with 1 KB messages, and its p99 is lower too: 7.5 to 21 ms against Queen's 47 to 76 at
3,000,000 msg/s, and 9.4 to 19 ms against 26 to 47 with 1 KB messages at 1,000,000 msg/s. On one
topic of 1,000 partitions it carried about 8,000,000 msg/s with a p99 near 12 ms, where Queen
carried 3,500,000. At 10,000 partitions Kafka needs about twice Queen's CPU; at 25,000 it still
carries the load, with a p99 of 105 ms on 57 cores, and at 50,000 and 100,000 it does not.
Queen's latency and CPU stay flat from 10 to 10,000,000 partitions, on 14 to 23 cores.
Queen answers a write after two fsyncs; Kafka, as we ran it, once its replicas hold the write in
memory. All numbers are from 2026-10-14; the runs are linked at the end.

Kafka's numbers on this page are on XFS, with its thread pools sized for the 24-vCPU brokers. Our
first runs had it on ext4, where every append could wait for the filesystem's journal and its p99
was up to ten times higher; [Kafka and the filesystem](#kafka-and-the-filesystem) has the
measurements and the cause.

## The machines

Six VMs in one GCP zone (`europe-west1-b`) and one VPC, Ubuntu 24.04. Both systems ran on the
same three brokers, one system at a time, with nothing else on them.

| Role | Machine type | CPU | Memory | Disk for the data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 brokers | `c4-standard-24-lssd` | 24 vCPU, Intel Xeon 6985P-C | 88 GB | the four local NVMe disks of the machine type (4 × 375 GB) in one RAID0: ext4 for Queen, XFS for Kafka, both `noatime` |
| 3 load generators | `c4-highcpu-16` | 16 vCPU, Intel Xeon Platinum 8581C | 32 GB | boot disk only |

We measured every broker's disks before giving them roles, because one slow disk on a follower
becomes the p99 of every client of that follower. On all three: a 32 KB write followed by
`fdatasync` took 0.055 ms at the median and 0.21 ms at p99, about 16,000 a second; sequential 1 MB
writes ran at 1,340 MB/s, which is GCP's limit of about 335 to 350 MB/s for each local disk, four
times. The boot disk (Hyperdisk Balanced, about 700 syncs a second on the 16-vCPU machines we
tried first) holds nothing of either system's data.

## How we ran it

- **The load.** Nine load processes, three on each load generator, open loop: each process
  keeps its schedule when the broker slows down, so a stall shows up as latency instead of
  lowering the load. Producers send 100 messages a request to one partition, in turn over all the
  partitions. Each Queen process talks to one of the three brokers; Kafka's clients talk to each
  partition's leader.
- **The messages.** JSON events of 256 bytes, or about 1,027 bytes in the 1 KB runs, the same
  generator on both sides.
- **Consumers.** One consumer group. 9 consumers in all for 10 partitions, 99 for 100 and 297 for
  every larger count, the same on both sides. From 1,000 partitions Queen's consumers pop up to 10
  partitions and 1,000 messages at a time (one partition and up to 20,000 or 5,000 messages with 10
  and 100 partitions) and acknowledge without waiting; Kafka's poll up to 1,000 records. On ext4,
  Kafka polling 20,000 and 5,000 records at 10 and 100 partitions did not read better.
- **A point.** A fresh cluster, the queue or topic created with all its partitions and warmed
  before the timed run, then 70 seconds of load, the first 10 a ramp. No system was measured
  while it created partitions.
- **Latency.** End to end, from the moment a message was scheduled to be sent to the moment a
  consumer received it. Each load process keeps a histogram for every 10-second window; for each
  window we take the slowest of the nine processes. The tables give the median of the three
  windows in seconds 30 to 60 and, in brackets, the worst of them (the statistic of our other
  benchmark pages). The figures use the median: one spike in one window is shown in the brackets
  and does not decide the line.
- **Carried.** A point is carried when nothing is shed, nothing errs and at least 99% of what was
  pushed is consumed.
- **Resources.** CPU is the three brokers' cores added up over the loaded window. Memory is the
  resident memory of the busiest broker. Disk is the mean write rate of a broker's data volume.
  Each broker and loader was sampled every one or two seconds.
- **One run a point.** We ran most points once. Repeats on the same machines moved Queen's p99 by
  a few milliseconds in the median window and up to 20 ms in the worst one; on ext4 Kafka's worst
  window moved more, which is one reason for the median.

## The settings

| | Queen 2.3.0 | Kafka 4.3.1 |
|---|---|---|
| Build | the released image `ghcr.io/queen-mq/queen:2.3.0` (digest `967fc40c`), its defaults | the Apache release, JDK 21, KRaft with the three brokers as controllers |
| Durability | three replicas in one raft group; a write is answered after the leader and one follower have fsynced it | three replicas, `acks=all`, `min.insync.replicas=2`; a write is answered when the in-sync replicas hold it in memory, with no fsync |
| Producer | binary push body compressed with zstd | idempotent, lz4, `linger.ms=5`, 4 producers a process, batches up to 1 MiB (franz-go) |
| Consumer | framed pops, acknowledgements answered as counts | the KIP-848 consumer protocol, `fetch.max.wait.ms=500`, `fetch.min.bytes=1` |
| Broker | deduplication off, completed messages kept 1,800 s (nothing deleted during a point); 10,000,000 partitions with `QUEEN_CONSUME_IDLE_UNLOAD_S=30` | 6 GB heap with G1 and a 20 ms pause goal; 24 request handler threads (`num.io.threads`), 12 network threads, 12 replica fetchers; retention 10 minutes |
| Filesystem and page cache | ext4 `noatime`; background writeback from 5% of memory | XFS (`mkfs.xfs` defaults) `noatime`; background writeback from 1 GiB (`vm.dirty_background_bytes`) |

Both ran with the same kernel settings otherwise (`vm.swappiness=1`, `vm.dirty_ratio=60`,
`vm.min_free_kbytes=524288`, 1,048,576 open files, larger socket buffers). Queen does not depend
on the last row: it fsyncs what it writes, so its dirty pages stay under 0.1 GB. We ran it on XFS
too, and on ext4 with `lazytime`, and neither helped. At 1,000 partitions and 3,000,000 msg/s
Queen read 20 / 62 ms on XFS against 21 / 47, 22 / 47 and 21 / 50 ms in three runs on ext4, and
22 / 47 ms with `lazytime`; with 1 KB messages at 1,000,000 msg/s it read 9.4 / 26 ms on both
filesystems, and a 32 KB write and fsync ran the same on XFS, about 16,500 a second at a median of
0.05 ms. Kafka's thread pools are larger than in our September comparison and on 2026-10-13
(16 request handlers, 8 network threads and 8 replica fetchers, sized for 16-vCPU brokers); its
other settings are the same as there.

## Kafka and the filesystem

Our first Kafka runs used the brokers' ext4 as it came, with `noatime`, and our hosts' background
writeback from 5% of memory: on 88 GB brokers about 3.6 GB of dirty pages, written out at the
RAID's limit about every 12 seconds. Kafka's p99 came in bursts. At 3,000,000 msg/s it read 938 ms
at 10 partitions and 43 to 96 ms from 100 to 10,000 in the median window, with worst windows up to
1,401 ms; at 2,000,000 msg/s on 1,000 partitions it read 71 and 95 ms in the worst window of two
runs, against 25 ms on the DigitalOcean machines of
[the partition page](/benchmarks/partitions/#kafka-on-the-same-machines). In every 10-second
window all nine load processes on all three load generators read the same p99, and the producers'
p99 equalled the end-to-end p99: a stall on the write path of the whole cluster at once, with the
brokers at 4 of 24 cores.

A sampler listed every 20 ms the Kafka threads blocked in the kernel. Request handlers and replica
fetchers stopped for up to 300 ms at a time, nearly always in the same place: a `write()` updating
the file's modification time (`ext4_dirty_inode`) waited for write access to an inode block that
the journal was committing (`jbd2_journal_get_write_access`), and the commit waited behind the
page cache writeback queued on the local disks, 50 to 190 ms a write in the worst seconds. So
every append could wait for a journal commit, and the commit for the disks.

We changed one thing at a time, a fresh cluster for every run. Background writeback from 1 GiB
writes the page cache out in smaller steps; it lowered the worst windows but not every median.
Mounting ext4 with `lazytime` keeps timestamp updates in memory, so an append no longer touches
the journal: it removed most of the stall (2,000,000 msg/s on 1,000 partitions went to 6.3 / 9.9
ms in the worst window). XFS, with the thread pools sized for 24 cores, removed the spikes that
were left. We changed the filesystem and the thread pools together, so these runs do not split
that last step between them. Kafka's p99 at 3,000,000 msg/s, the median of the three windows
(worst window in brackets):

| Partitions | ext4, writeback from 5% | ext4, from 1 GiB | ext4 `lazytime`, 1 GiB | XFS, 1 GiB, threads for 24 vCPU |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 938 (1,401) | 506 (514) | 272 (350) | 11 (45) |
| 100 | 96 (103) | 50 (61) | 13 (15) | 7.5 (8) |
| 1,000 | 43 (148) | 88 (104) | 15 (29) | 8.1 (8) |
| 10,000 | 46 (138) | 61 (110) | 34 (46) | 21 (78) |

<Figure id="benchmarks/gcp-kafka-writeback" />

If you run Kafka on machines like these, put its log directories on XFS, as Kafka's documentation
recommends, or at least mount ext4 with `lazytime`, and bound background writeback
(`vm.dirty_background_bytes`) so the page cache goes to disk in small steps. Queen is not affected
by either setting, because it fsyncs what it writes (see [the settings](#the-settings)).

## Kafka with an fsync on every append

Queen answers a write after two fsyncs; Kafka above answers when the replicas hold it in memory. To
see what durability costs Kafka we ran it once more with `log.flush.interval.messages=1`, an fsync
of every append on the leader and on the followers before it counts, everything else as above.
p50 / p99 in milliseconds (worst window in brackets) and the cores of the three brokers:

| Load | Kafka with fsync | cores | Kafka without | cores | Queen | cores |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3,000,000 msg/s, 100 partitions | 7.5 / 12 (13) | 29.3 | 6.0 / 7.5 (8) | 18.5 | 21 / 54 (65) | 23.0 |
| 3,000,000 msg/s, 1,000 partitions | 7.6 / 198 (285) | 30.2 | 6.0 / 8.1 (8) | 21.0 | 21 / 47 (56) | 22.4 |
| 3,000,000 msg/s, 10,000 partitions | 95 / 281 (301) | 39.1 | 8.4 / 21 (78) | 52.8 | 24 / 61 (66) | 23.3 |
| 1,000,000 msg/s of 1 KB, 1,000 partitions | 8.1 / 11 (11) | 15.9 | 7.4 / 9.4 (9) | 11.9 | 9.4 / 26 (27) | 16.1 |

Every point was carried. With few partitions durability costs Kafka little: 1.5 ms on the median
and 4 ms on the p99 at 100 partitions, still well under Queen. The cost grows with the partition
count, because Kafka syncs a file per partition where Queen syncs one log: at 1,000 partitions
with 256-byte messages Kafka's p99 swung from 89 to 440 ms over the first 50 seconds and settled at
12 ms in the last two windows, and at 10,000 partitions Queen reads 24 / 61 ms against Kafka's
95 / 281 on fewer cores. The disks wrote about twice the bytes (978 against 435 MB/s a broker at
100 partitions): a page that is still filling is written again on every flush. Each point ran once,
and Kafka's ceiling with an fsync on every append was not measured.

## Kafka in queue mode

Every Kafka number above uses a consumer group: each partition goes to one consumer, which commits
one offset for everything it has read. Queen keeps a lease and an ack for every message. Kafka has
a mode that does the same: a share group ([KIP-932](https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/display/KAFKA/KIP-932%3A+Queues+for+Kafka)),
where the partition's leader locks each record for the consumer that fetched it (30 s by
default), takes an ack per record, redelivers what is released or times out, and writes that state
to an internal topic through a share coordinator. Several consumers read one partition at once, so
a share group keeps no order within a partition; Queen gives a partition to one worker at a time.
We ran it at 3,000,000 msg/s with the same 297 consumers as the consumer-group runs, which needs
`group.share.max.size` raised from its default of 200 to 300. Each consumer accepts every record it
polls, and the acks travel with its next fetch. p50 / p99 in milliseconds (worst window in
brackets) and the cores of the three brokers:

| Partitions | Kafka, share group | cores | Kafka, consumer group | cores | Queen | cores |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 6.3 / 9.3 (9) | 24.6 | 6.0 / 7.5 (8) | 18.5 | 21 / 54 (65) | 23.0 |
| 1,000 | 6.4 / 23 (23) | 35.0 | 6.0 / 8.1 (8) | 21.0 | 21 / 47 (56) | 22.4 |
| 10,000 | 78 / 163 (206) | 52.2 | 8.4 / 21 (78) | 52.8 | 24 / 61 (66) | 23.3 |

<Figure id="benchmarks/gcp-3m-modes-p99" />

<Figure id="benchmarks/gcp-3m-modes-p50" />

<Figure id="benchmarks/gcp-3m-modes-cpu" />

Every point was carried. Keeping a state for every record costs Kafka CPU first: 6 more cores at
100 partitions and 14 more at 1,000, where it uses half again Queen's. At 1,000 partitions its p99
nearly triples, to 23 ms, while its median stays near 6 ms. At 10,000 partitions the share group
reads 78 / 163 ms, three times Queen's 24 / 61, on more than twice Queen's cores. Kafka still
answers a write from memory here, where Queen waits for two fsyncs. Each point ran once, and the
share group was not run at 10 partitions or above 10,000.

## Where each one leads

Kafka, run as on this page, leads up to 10,000 partitions: on the median, by three to six times
with 256-byte messages and by a tenth to a quarter with 1 KB (by half at 10 partitions); on the
p99, 7.5 to 21 ms against Queen's 47 to 76, and 9.4 to 19 ms against 26 to 47 with 1 KB messages;
on CPU up to 1,000 partitions; and on throughput, about 8,000,000 msg/s on 1,000 partitions where
Queen carried 3,500,000. Queen leads on CPU from 10,000 partitions and on everything from 50,000.
Kafka needed about twice Queen's CPU at 10,000, 56 to 57 cores at 25,000 for a p99 of 45 and 105
ms, and did not carry 50,000, where Queen keeps the same latency on the same 14 to 23 cores up to 10,000,000. Queen also
answers a write only after two disks have it. With an fsync on every append Kafka keeps its lead at
100 partitions and with 1 KB messages at 1,000, loses its steadiness at 1,000 with 256-byte
messages, and falls behind Queen at 10,000 ([the runs](#kafka-with-an-fsync-on-every-append)).
In queue mode, with a lock and an ack per record as Queen keeps, Kafka keeps its median up to
1,000 partitions, needs more CPU than Queen from 1,000, and at 10,000 is three times slower than
Queen ([the runs](#kafka-in-queue-mode)).

## What these runs do not show

- Kafka's ceiling with an fsync on every append, and repeats of its fsync points (one run each).
- Kafka on XFS with the thread pools of 16-vCPU brokers, or ext4 with the larger pools: we changed
  the filesystem and the thread pools together.
- Repeated runs for every point: most points ran once.
- Kafka at 1,000,000 and 10,000,000 partitions, and Queen at 25,000 and 50,000.
- Kafka's share group at 10 partitions, above 10,000, with 1 KB messages, or at its ceiling, and
  with consumers that release or reject records.

We ran Queen 2.3.0 and Kafka 4.3.1 on the same three GCP brokers with local NVMe disks, with the
same load generators, the same load and the same report. At 3,000,000 msg/s of 256-byte messages
Queen carried every partition count from 10 to 10,000,000 with an end-to-end p99 between 47 and
78 ms, on 19 to 23 cores for the three brokers together. Kafka's median is three to six times
lower than Queen's wherever it carries the load. Its p99 matches Queen's at 100 partitions (61
against 65 ms), is about twice Queen's at 1,000 and 10,000, and 514 ms at 10 partitions; from
10,000 partitions it needs twice Queen's CPU, and at 100,000 it does not carry the rate. With
1 KB messages at 1,000,000 msg/s the medians come close (Kafka 7.5 to 9.8 ms, Queen 9.5 to 12),
while Queen's p99 stays at 27 to 34 ms and Kafka's at 98 to 136. All numbers are from
2026-10-14; the runs are linked at the end.

## The machines

Six VMs in one GCP zone (`europe-west1-b`) and one VPC, Ubuntu 24.04. Both systems ran on the
same three brokers, one system at a time, with nothing else on them.

| Role | Machine type | CPU | Memory | Disk for the data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 brokers | `c4-standard-24-lssd` | 24 vCPU, Intel Xeon 6985P-C | 88 GB | the four local NVMe disks of the machine type (4 × 375 GB) in one RAID0, ext4, `noatime` |
| 3 load generators | `c4-highcpu-16` | 16 vCPU, Intel Xeon Platinum 8581C | 32 GB | boot disk only |

We measured every broker's disks before giving them roles, because one slow disk on a follower
becomes the p99 of every client of that follower. On all three: a 32 KB write followed by
`fdatasync` took 0.055 ms at the median and 0.21 ms at p99, about 16,000 a second; sequential 1 MB
writes ran at 1,340 MB/s, which is GCP's limit of about 335 to 350 MB/s for each local disk, four
times. The boot disk (Hyperdisk Balanced, about 700 syncs a second on the 16-vCPU machines we
tried first) holds nothing of either system's data.

## How we ran it

- **The load.** Nine load processes, three on each load generator, open loop: each process
  keeps its schedule when the broker slows down, so a stall shows up as latency instead of
  lowering the load. Producers send 100 messages a request to one partition, in turn over all the
  partitions. Each Queen process talks to one of the three brokers; Kafka's clients talk to each
  partition's leader.
- **The messages.** JSON events of 256 bytes, or about 1,027 bytes in the 1 KB runs, the same
  generator on both sides.
- **Consumers.** One consumer group. 9 consumers in all for 10 partitions, 99 for 100 and 297 for
  every larger count, the same on both sides. From 1,000 partitions Queen's consumers pop up to 10
  partitions and 1,000 messages at a time (one partition and up to 20,000 or 5,000 messages with 10
  and 100 partitions) and acknowledge without waiting; Kafka's poll up to 1,000 records.
- **A point.** A fresh cluster, the queue or topic created with all its partitions and warmed
  before the timed run, then 70 seconds of load, the first 10 a ramp. No system was measured
  while it created partitions.
- **The figures.** End-to-end latency is from the moment a message was scheduled to be sent to
  the moment a consumer received it. The tables give the slowest load process's worst
  10-second window in seconds 30 to 60, the same statistic for both systems.
- **Carried.** A point is carried when nothing is shed, nothing errs and at least 99% of what was
  pushed is consumed.
- **Resources.** CPU is the three brokers' cores added up over the loaded window. Memory is the
  resident memory of the busiest broker. Disk is the mean write rate of a broker's data volume.
  Each broker and loader was sampled every one or two seconds.
- **One run a point.** We ran most points once. The 1,000-partition point at 3,000,000 msg/s ran
  twice for Queen on the same machines, with a p99 of 56 and of 78 ms by this statistic: read a
  difference of 20 ms between two cells as noise.

## The settings

| | Queen 2.3.0 | Kafka 4.3.1 |
|---|---|---|
| Build | the released image `ghcr.io/queen-mq/queen:2.3.0` (digest `967fc40c`), its defaults | the Apache release, JDK 21, KRaft with the three brokers as controllers |
| Durability | three replicas in one raft group; a write is answered after the leader and one follower have fsynced it | three replicas, `acks=all`, `min.insync.replicas=2`; a write is answered when the in-sync replicas hold it in memory, with no fsync |
| Producer | binary push body compressed with zstd | idempotent, lz4, `linger.ms=5`, 4 producers a process, batches up to 1 MiB (franz-go) |
| Consumer | framed pops, acknowledgements answered as counts | the KIP-848 consumer protocol, `fetch.max.wait.ms=500`, `fetch.min.bytes=1` |
| Broker | deduplication off, completed messages kept 1,800 s (nothing deleted during a point); 10,000,000 partitions with `QUEEN_CONSUME_IDLE_UNLOAD_S=30` | 6 GB heap with G1 and a 20 ms pause goal; 16 request handler threads, 8 network threads, 8 replica fetchers; retention 10 minutes |
| Page cache | background writeback from 5% of memory | background writeback from 1 GiB (`vm.dirty_background_bytes`), see below |

Both ran with the same kernel settings otherwise (`vm.swappiness=1`, `vm.dirty_ratio=60`,
`vm.min_free_kbytes=524288`, 1,048,576 open files, larger socket buffers). Kafka's settings are the ones of our September
comparison and of 2026-10-13, sized then for 16-vCPU brokers; we checked that none of them held
Kafka back here. At 1,000 partitions and 3,000,000 msg/s a Kafka broker used 6 to 10 of its 24
cores, its busiest request handler thread 28% of a core and its busiest network thread 45%; the
heap held at most 380 MB after a collection, and the collector paused for about 0.3 s in a whole
run, never more than 9 ms at a time.

Kafka's median is three to six times lower than Queen's at every count it carries: it answers a
write when the replicas hold it in memory, where Queen waits for two fsyncs. Its p99 is the same
as Queen's at 100 partitions and about twice Queen's at 1,000 and 10,000, and its tail comes in
bursts: one load process at 1,000 partitions read 12 to 146 ms from one 10-second window to the
next, where Queen's windows at that point stayed between 37 and 54 ms. At 10 partitions
each of Kafka's nine consumers reads 333,000 messages a second and its p99 settles at 300 to
470 ms a window. From 10,000 partitions Kafka pays for every partition: 47 cores at 10,000, and at
100,000 it pushed 200,000 of the 3,000,000 messages a second offered. Its topic of 100,000
partitions took about six minutes to create; we did not run a million.

Queen's CPU, memory and disk do not follow the partition count. A broker's resident memory goes
from 11 GB at 10 partitions to 16 GB at a million and 25 GB at ten million, and each broker wrote
713 to 958 MB/s, under the disks' 1,340. The 100,000-partition point is marked not carried by
the rule because one pop of several million timed out (HTTP 503) in the first seconds of the load;
every other number at that point is in line with its neighbours.

## Kafka and the page cache

Kafka writes into the page cache and leaves the writing to disk to the kernel. Our hosts start the
background writeback when dirty pages reach 5% of memory. On 88 GB brokers that is about 3.6 GB:
the dirty pages built up to that, and the kernel then wrote them out at 1,270 to 1,330 MB/s,
the RAID's limit, about every 12 seconds. On the 31 GB machines of 2026-10-13 the same setting
kept about 1 GB dirty and the writes smooth. With the writeback starting at 1 GiB the bursts
stopped up to 1,000 partitions (at most 700 to 800 MB/s; at 10,000 the dirty pages still reached
2 GB), and Kafka's p99 fell:

<Figure id="benchmarks/gcp-kafka-writeback" />

The tables on this page use Kafka's better setting. Queen does not depend on it: it fsyncs what
it writes, so at 1,000 partitions its dirty pages never passed 0.1 GB, under either threshold. Queen's numbers here are
with the 5% default; one point at 1,000 partitions repeated with 1 GiB showed no gain.

At 2,000,000 msg/s with the 5% setting, Kafka's p99 here was 74 ms at 100 partitions and 71 ms at
1,000, against 43 and 25 ms on the DigitalOcean machines of
[the partition page](/benchmarks/partitions/#kafka-on-the-same-machines). Its median was lower
here (6 ms against 8 to 9). With 1 GiB some of the difference in the tail remains, and we have
not found where.

Larger messages bring the two medians close: Kafka's is 7.5 to 9.8 ms, Queen's 9.5 to 12 ms from 100
partitions up. The tails stay apart: Queen's p99 is 27 to 34 ms from 100 to 1,000,000 partitions,
Kafka's 98 to 136 ms, three to five times higher, and 553 ms at 10 partitions. Kafka needs less CPU
up to 1,000 partitions (8 to 12 cores against 14 to 17), twice Queen's at 10,000, and it does not
carry 100,000. The two 65 ms cells of Queen at 10 and 10,000,000 partitions are each one load
process's one window; over all nine processes the last 30 seconds read 45 and 28 ms. Each Queen
broker wrote 665 to 732 MB/s and each Kafka broker about 610 MB/s: both compress these JSON
messages, Queen with zstd and Kafka with lz4. A Queen broker held 5 to 9 GB of memory up to a
million partitions and 18 GB at ten million.

## The ceiling at 1,000 partitions

How far one queue of 1,000 partitions goes on these brokers, Queen 2.3.0, a fresh cluster for
each rate:

| msg/s | e2e p50 / p99 | cores | disk writes a broker |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2,000,000 | 6 / 18 | 20.0 | 610 MB/s |
| 2,500,000 | 9 / 27 | 20.8 | 680 MB/s |
| 3,000,000 | 21 / 78 | 22.2 | 760 MB/s |
| 3,500,000 | 47 / 98 | 26.2 | 865 MB/s |
| 4,000,000 | not carried: 3,950,000 pushed, 1.2 s | 34.6 | 960 MB/s |

At 4,000,000 msg/s neither the disks (960 of 1,340 MB/s) nor the leader's CPU (16 of 24 cores)
was full; what stopped it is not profiled yet. On the smaller `c4-standard-16-lssd` (16 vCPU,
two local disks, 670 MB/s) the same queue carried 2,000,000 msg/s at a p99 of 60 ms, 2,250,000 at
400 ms and not 2,500,000, with the disks writing 550 to 620 MB/s of their 670.

## Where each one leads

Kafka leads on the median, by three to six times, and on CPU up to 1,000 partitions. If your
partition count is in the hundreds and a few milliseconds at the median matter more than the
p99, Kafka on these machines is the faster broker. Queen leads on the tail from 1,000 partitions
up, on CPU from 10,000, and it is the only one of the two that carries 100,000 partitions and
more: at ten million its p99 is 54 ms on 23 cores. It also answers a write only after two disks
have it, which Kafka in this configuration does not.

## What these runs do not show

- Kafka with an fsync on every write (`flush.messages=1`), which would compare durability for
  durability. We ran Kafka as it is usually run.
- Kafka's thread pools sized for 24 cores. They were not saturated at the counts it carried.
- Repeated runs for every point: most points ran once.
- Kafka at 1,000,000 and 10,000,000 partitions.

Few partitions in one queue is the shape that suits a broker with a log per partition best: few
logs, full batches, little state to keep. So that is where we raised the rate until each system
stopped. Queen 2.3.0 carries 1,000,000 messages a second here with an end-to-end p99 of 19 ms and
2,000,000 with 63 ms. Kafka, on the same machines the same day, read 11 ms and 43 ms and went on
to 4,000,000, where Queen does not go: 2,500,000 is the first rate it did not carry (2026-10-13).
[Compare](/start/compare/) explains where each system is better.

## The shape

One queue, or one topic, of 100 partitions. Producers pushed 256-byte JSON messages, 100 at a time
to one partition, and consumers acknowledged asynchronously. Every point started from a fresh
cluster with the partitions already created, ran its load for 90 seconds (Kafka 70), and the
figures cover seconds 30 to 60 of it.

Queen ran on 2026-10-12 and 13 as pre-release builds of 2.3.0, with the wire forms that release
adds and that a client turns on: the [binary push with compressed groups, framed pops](/start/clients/#binary-push-push-compression-and-framed-pop)
and acks answered with counts. Deduplication was off and nothing was deleted while a point ran,
as for Kafka. Kafka 4.3.1 ran on 2026-10-13 on the same three brokers with its September settings
(three replicas, `acks=all`, no fsync). Runs and scripts:
[`benchmark-queen/2026-10-12-three-node-new-forms/`](https://github.com/queen-mq/queen/tree/master/benchmark-queen/2026-10-12-three-node-new-forms)
(Queen: `runs/grid-final.jsonl`) and `benchmark-queen/2026-09-30-kafka-pulsar/runs/kafka/` (the
`-oct13` folders).

|---|---|---|
| 100,000 | 3 / 6 | not run |
| 500,000 | 5 / 9 | not run |
| 1,000,000 | 10 / 19 | 8 / 11 |
| 1,500,000 | 20 / 41 | not run |
| 2,000,000 | 31 / 63 | 8 / 43 |

## 2.3.0 against 2.2.0

The same machines also ran release 2.2.0 with its JSON forms next to the first build of the new
ones, on 2026-10-12: one queue of 200 partitions, a 60-second deduplication window, completed
messages deleted after 60 seconds, one cluster stepped through the rates, all load processes
pooled. The fixes of the night that followed are not in this pair.

| | 2.2.0 | 2.3.0 pre-release |
|---|---|---|
| 600,000 msg/s: e2e p50 / p99 | 12 / 43 ms | 9 / 15 ms |
| 1,000,000 msg/s: e2e p50 / p99 | 27 / 85 ms | 12 / 18 ms |
| 1,000,000 msg/s: cores, three brokers | 16.4 | 13.4 |
| 1,000,000 msg/s: cores, load machines | 14.4 | 8.3 |
| 1,500,000 msg/s: e2e p50 / p99 | 77 / 213 ms | 26 / 58 ms |
| Most it pushed when offered more | 1.57M msg/s | 2.45M msg/s |

A binary push body costs the broker and the client less than JSON, a client's compressed batch is
stored as it arrives, and a pop is answered with the stored records as they are. The 600,000 and
1,500,000 rows and the load machines' cores are the build before the deduplication filter fix
(`4f8119304`), the rest the build with it (`1d0981c57`).

## The ceiling

Queen carried 2,000,000 msg/s at every partition count from 10 to 10,000,000
([partition count](/benchmarks/partitions/)). At 2,500,000, run on 1,000 partitions, it took every
push and its consumers took 2.35M a second, 94% of the rate, with an e2e of 101 / 198 ms: not
carried. On 200 partitions with deduplication on, a cluster offered more than it can take pushed
2.45M a second.

What stops it is the planner. One planning cycle runs at a time on the leader, and at the limit
there is always one running: 238 cycles a second of 4.2 ms, about 26,000 pushes of 100 messages.
The leader's disk is then at 24 to 34% and the leader at 10 of its 16 cores; no thread is out of
CPU. Kafka carried 4,000,000 msg/s in and out at 13 / 243 ms on 21.8 cores, and we did not push it
further.

## Where Queen's latency comes from

A push is answered once its entry is fsynced on two of the three nodes. Two more things are
Queen's own, and both are choices we would make again.

A pop is a write. The consumption engine on the leader hands out a lease, and the answer waits
until the checkpoint carrying that lease has committed through the log. Since 2.3.0 that
checkpoint is taken at once when an answer waits and none is in flight
(`QUEEN_CONSUME_CHECKPOINT_EAGER`), and at the latest every 5 ms
(`QUEEN_CONSUME_CHECKPOINT_MS`). A new leader therefore knows every lease the old one gave, and no
consumer is ever handed a message that another consumer still holds. A message pays for two
replicated commits between its push and its delivery. `QUEEN_CONSUME_FAST=1` answers before the
checkpoint and gives that guarantee up; no run here used it.

One leader orders every write of a raft group. That leader is what makes a transaction one entry in
one log, and it is also where the work queues up as the rate climbs: at 1M msg/s it used 6.7 of its
16 cores while the two followers used 4.2 each. Kafka spreads its partition leaders over all three
brokers. A cluster can run several raft groups (`QUEEN_RAFT_GROUPS`), each with its own leader, and
a tenant lives in exactly one. Every run in the tables above used one tenant, so one group and one
leader. Three tenants in three groups carried 2,500,000 msg/s over 500,000 partitions where one
group stopped near 2.15M, for 38 to 50% more broker CPU at the same rate (2026-10-12, a 60-second
deduplication window).

## Deduplication and retention on

The tables above have deduplication off and delete nothing, because Kafka has no equivalent to
price in. With a 60-second deduplication window and completed messages deleted after 60 seconds,
on the last pre-release build of the night (2026-10-13, 200 seconds a point, the last 60 pooled
over all load processes):

| Partitions | 1,500,000 msg/s, both on | The same load, both off |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | not carried: 1.32M pushed | 20 / 54 |
| 100 | 25 / 45 | 20 / 40 |
| 1,000 | 19 / 36 | 17 / 35 |
| 10,000 | 27 / 48 | 17 / 36 |
| 100,000 | 28 / 51 | 18 / 36 |
| 1,000,000 | 21 / 40 | 17 / 34 |
| 10,000,000 | not carried after 100 s: memory | 19 / 34 |

At 1,000,000 msg/s on 1,000 partitions the two conditions read alike: 12 / 20 ms, level for 150
seconds. Two points do not hold. Ten partitions at 150,000 msg/s each: the planner's
deduplication lookups for a partition that busy take the cycle from 0.2 to 6 ms as the window
fills. Ten million partitions: a minute of deduplication state and outcomes on top of ten million
partitions needs more than the 31 GB of these brokers. And 2,000,000 msg/s with both on holds on
1,000 partitions, where its e2e median rose from 31 to 56 ms over six minutes, and on 100,000
(63 / 106 ms after three minutes). On 1,000 partitions it rose over six minutes in each of the
three conditions we ran, with deletes and without, so read the 2,000,000 row of the first table as
a minute, not as an hour.

At 10,000 and 100,000 partitions the higher p99 is the store's checkpoint, which cannot keep its
250 ms pace when every changed row sits in a different page. It is not fixed in 2.3.0.

## When a request carries one message

Every run above pushes 100 messages to one partition per request, and the broker's work grows
with the commands it plans more than with the messages they carry. With one message a request, on
1,000 partitions with deduplication and retention on (2026-10-12, 90-second steps on one cluster):

| Requests a second | Push p50 / p99 | e2e p50 / p99 | Cores, three brokers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25,000 | 3 / 5 ms | 6 / 12 ms | 16.1 |
| 50,000 | 5 / 13 ms | 11 / 28 ms | 20.0 |
| 75,000 | 8 / 22 ms | 30 / 61 ms | 24.0 |
| 100,000 | 12 / 28 ms | 124 / 174 ms | 25.3 |

Without deduplication and deletes the hard limit is about 145,000 requests a second. The limit is
the apply stage: a single-message request is one command, and every command writes its own rows.
Plan for it if your producers send one message per entity at a time, and batch where you can.

## The four systems in September

Redpanda and Pulsar have not been run again, so this is the comparison of 2026-09-30 and 10-01 as
it was measured: Queen 2.0.0-beta.2 with its JSON forms, Kafka 4.3.1, Redpanda 26.2.3 and Pulsar
4.2.4, one queue or topic of 200 partitions, 60 seconds a point with the figures over the last 30,
on other machines of the same size. Queen's column is that old build: read it for the other three
systems, and take Queen's numbers from the tables above. Runs:
`benchmark-queen/2026-09-30-stage03-3node/` (Queen, tags `mb2c-q1-t200-*`) and
[`benchmark-queen/2026-09-30-kafka-pulsar/runs/`](https://github.com/queen-mq/queen/tree/master/benchmark-queen/2026-09-30-kafka-pulsar/runs)
(the others).

What each system had done when it answered a producer:

| System | Copies | Acknowledged after |
|---|---|---|
| Queen | 3 | the entry is fsynced on 2 of the 3 nodes |
| Kafka | 3 (`acks=all`, `min.insync.replicas=2`) | every in-sync replica has the batch, in page cache; no fsync |
| Redpanda | 3 | 2 of 3 have fsynced (`write_caching` off, its default) |
| Pulsar | 3 (ensemble 3, write quorum 3, ack quorum 2) | 2 bookies have fsynced their journal |

Pulsar waits for the same two fsyncs as Queen and stayed flat near 20 ms up to 2M, the highest
rate it ran; that is the line 2.3.0 now reads next to at 1M and has not been measured against on
the same machines. Redpanda's median stays low and its p99 sits near 200 ms. Its README in the
archive traces that tail to one broker, the one with CPU steal and the slowest disk, and notes
that Redpanda is tested on XFS while that rig had one ext4 disk per broker. Redpanda fell behind
at 3M.

## What this page does not show

The released binary: the matrix ran on the builds of the two days before the release, and 2.3.0
itself has not been run through it. A million a second for hours: each point is 90 seconds, the
longest runs on this code are six minutes, and our runs of hours are at 500,000 msg/s on
2.0.0-beta.1 ([soak](/benchmarks/soak/)). Clients with the default JSON forms at these rates: the
2.2.0 column above is the nearest measurement. Nor does it show Redpanda or Pulsar against 2.3.0,
payloads other than 256 bytes, or consumers that do any work.

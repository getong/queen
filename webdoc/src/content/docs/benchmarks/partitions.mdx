This is the test Queen was built for. Give every entity its own partition and your partition
count is your entity count: a million customers, ten million orders. On three 16-vCPU
DigitalOcean nodes we offered one queue from 100,000 to 2,000,000 messages a second at partition
counts from 10 to 10,000,000. Pre-release builds of Queen 2.3.0 carried every one of the 35 points,
and the count hardly shows: at 1,000,000 msg/s the end-to-end p99 is 18 to 21 ms from 10 to 100,000
partitions, 32 ms at a million and 38 ms at ten million (2026-10-12 and 13). Kafka, on the same
machines at 2,000,000 msg/s, is ahead up to 1,000 partitions, behind at 10,000, and does not carry
100,000. On three GCP nodes with 24 vCPU and local NVMe, the released 2.3.0 carried 3,000,000 msg/s
at every count from 10 to 10,000,000 with a p99 of 47 to 78 ms in the worst window; Kafka there was
faster up to 10,000 partitions and did not carry 50,000
([Queen vs Kafka on NVMe](/benchmarks/queen-vs-kafka/)). [Compare](/start/compare/) explains how
each system gets order per entity.

## The shape

The load is the one on [throughput and latency](/benchmarks/throughput/): 256-byte JSON messages
pushed 100 at a time to one partition, a fresh cluster for every point, 90 seconds of load (Kafka
70), figures over seconds 30 to 60. Every partition existed before the timed run: Queen's load
generator pushed one message into each partition and drained it, and Kafka's topic was created and
warmed first. No system was measured while it created partitions. Queen ran as pre-release builds
of 2.3.0 with the binary push, compressed groups, framed pops and counted acks, deduplication off
and nothing deleted during a point; 9 consumers read 10 partitions, 99 read 100, and 297 read
every larger count with pops of up to 10 partitions and 1,000 messages. The ten-million column ran
with `QUEEN_CONSUME_IDLE_UNLOAD_S=30`. Runs:
[`benchmark-queen/2026-10-12-three-node-new-forms/`](https://github.com/queen-mq/queen/tree/master/benchmark-queen/2026-10-12-three-node-new-forms)
(`runs/grid-final.jsonl`, and `NIGHT.md` for what each build fixed); Kafka's are the `-oct13`
folders of `benchmark-queen/2026-09-30-kafka-pulsar/runs/kafka/`.

<Figure id="benchmarks/partitions-memory" />

That is about 1 KB per partition on every node, and a little more on the leader, which also
holds each consumer group's state for the partitions it has read. Memory is what sets the
partition ceiling: ten million partitions took more than a third of a 31 GB node at 100,000 msg/s,
and more than half at 2,000,000. A leader keeps a group's state for a partition
`QUEEN_CONSUME_IDLE_UNLOAD_S` after its last use (600 s by default); the ten-million column ran
with 30 s, which took 6 GB off the leader at 1,000,000 msg/s and cost nothing we could measure
(e2e 11 / 18 ms with either).

## Why the line stays flat

In Queen a partition is a few rows in the store, which every node keeps in memory: the partition
itself, its offsets, and a cursor for each consumer group that reads it. Its messages are records
in its queue's log, beside every other partition's, and one fsync covers everything a group of
entries wrote to that log. So a new partition adds memory and nothing else, neither a file nor a
replication stream nor an fsync of its own, and the leader's CPU at ten million partitions is what
it was at a thousand.

Kafka keeps a log of its own for every partition on every replica, with its own files and its own
place in replication; Redpanda runs a raft group per partition; Pulsar keeps a managed ledger per
partition in BookKeeper. That machinery is cheap with a few hundred partitions and grows with every
one you add, which is what Kafka's cores show from 10,000 on.

Before 2.3.0 the line was flat and higher: 2.0.0-beta.2 read a p99 of 103 to 163 ms at 1,000,000
msg/s from 200 to ten million partitions, and before the fixes of this release ten million
partitions did not carry 1,500,000. The release removed the costs that showed at high counts: the
leader's scan for idle consumer state, which held 625,000 partitions under one lock; a table of
partition names that hashed every name again each time it doubled; and the merge of the queue
log's directory, synced in one go.

## With deduplication and retention on

The matrix has deduplication off and deletes nothing. With a 60-second deduplication window and
completed messages deleted after 60 seconds, 1,500,000 msg/s reads 19 to 28 ms at the median and
36 to 51 ms at p99 from 100 to a million partitions. It is not carried on 10 partitions (the
planner, with deduplication at 150,000 msg/s a partition) nor on ten million (memory: a minute of
deduplication state and outcomes on top of ten million partitions needs more than these 31 GB
brokers have). The table is on
[throughput and latency](/benchmarks/throughput/#deduplication-and-retention-on).

## Many queues

A queue costs more than a partition. One million partitions spread over many queues, at the same
1M msg/s:

| Queues × partitions | In / out | e2e p50 / p99 |
|---|---|---|
| 1 × 1,000,000 | 1.00M / 1.00M | 46 / 123 ms |
| 1,000 × 1,000 | 1.00M / 1.00M | 95 / 216 ms |
| 10,000 × 100 | 1.00M / 1.01M | 185 / 561 ms; 76 pops timed out at their 2 s deadline |

<Figure id="benchmarks/partitions-queues" />

The run at 10,000 queues was repeated the same day at 167 / 518 ms. There were 10,008 consumers for
10,000 queues, about one each, so a pop carried about 205 messages against about 450 with one
queue, half the pops came back empty, and an ack took 60 ms against 20. Kafka and Pulsar ran their
many-topic shapes at 10,000 and 100,000 partitions in total, so the totals differ by ten times or
more: with 1,000 topics of 10 partitions Kafka held 20 / 79 ms and Pulsar 8 / 34 ms, and with 1,000
topics of 100 partitions Kafka fell behind (p99 5.8 s).

These three runs are 2.0.0-beta.2, on 2026-10-01, and the shape has not been measured on 2.3.0.

## Keys hashed onto partitions

If all you need is order per key, Kafka and Pulsar can hash the keys onto a fixed set of
partitions, and they are very good at it. Kafka spread 10,000,000 keys over 1,000 partitions at
8 / 13 ms, Pulsar 10,000,000 keys over 48 partitions with a Key_Shared subscription at 12 / 21 ms,
and Redpanda, laid out as Kafka, at 64 / 293 ms. What a hash gives up is isolation: a slow key holds
up every key that shares its partition, and you cannot give a consumer one key or ask where one key
stands. Queen's partition per entity keeps that, and the tables above are what it costs.

## What this page does not show

Traffic on every partition at once. Each push carried 100 messages for one partition, so 20,000
pushes a second reached at most 1,200,000 partitions in a minute: all ten million were created,
written and drained in the prefill, and the timed load touched a part of them. Traffic on all of
them within a minute means pushes of a message or two per partition, the shape described on
[throughput and latency](/benchmarks/throughput/#when-a-request-carries-one-message).

Each point ran 90 seconds, and 2,000,000 msg/s is not level over six minutes (the long runs are on
[soak and failover](/benchmarks/soak/), on an older build). The matrix ran on the builds of the two
days before the release, not on the released binary. Kafka was not run at 1,000,000 msg/s by
partition count on these machines, nor above 100,000 partitions, and Redpanda and Pulsar were not
run against 2.3.0 at all.

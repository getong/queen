Queen replicates every write through raft, so we measure it as a cluster: three nodes, every
write fsynced on two of them before the client hears back, and a load generator that keeps its
schedule when the broker slows down, so a stall shows up as latency instead of quietly lowering
the load. Then we run the other systems on the same machines with the same load and the same
report. These pages hold what came out, each number with the conditions that make it true,
including the places where another system did better.

2.3.0 is the release these pages were measured again for. The released build ran against Kafka on
three GCP nodes with 24 vCPU and local NVMe disks on 2026-10-14
([Queen vs Kafka on NVMe](/benchmarks/queen-vs-kafka/)). The pages on throughput, partition count,
transactions and Kafka clients hold the runs of the two days before, on pre-release builds and
three DigitalOcean nodes with 16 vCPU, with Kafka on the same machines. Redpanda and Pulsar were
last run in September against Queen 2.0.0-beta.2, on other 16-vCPU machines. Every table is kept
and names its machines and build, and no number is compared across two sets of machines.

## Where Queen leads, and where it does not

Queen hardly notices the partition count. A partition costs every node about 1 KB of memory, and
its messages go into its queue's log next to every other partition's, so ten million partitions
are written and fsynced the way ten are. We are proudest of this result, because it is the reason
Queen exists: one partition per entity, as many entities as you have. Kafka gives each partition
a log and a replication state of its own. On the GCP nodes at 3,000,000 msg/s it used 53 cores
at 10,000 partitions against Queen's 23, and 57 at 25,000 for a p99 of 105 ms; at 50,000 and
100,000 it pushed 601,000 and 174,000 of the 3,000,000 messages a second offered. Queen ran the
same load over ten million partitions with a p99 of 46 ms on 23 cores. In September, at
1,000,000 msg/s, Pulsar and Redpanda fell behind from 50,000 partitions.

Up to 10,000 partitions Kafka answers sooner and goes further. At 3,000,000 msg/s its median was
6 to 8 ms where Queen's was 21 to 37, and its p99 7.5 to 21 ms where Queen's was 47 to 76; up to
1,000 partitions it used less CPU (15 to 21 cores against Queen's 19 to 23). With 1 KB messages
at 1,000,000 msg/s the medians come close (Kafka 7.4 to 8.8 ms, Queen 9.4 to 11 from 100
partitions up) and Kafka's p99 is still the lower one, 9.4 to 19 ms against 26 to 47. On one topic
of 1,000 partitions Kafka carried about 8,000,000 msg/s at a p99 near 12 ms; one Queen queue
carried 3,500,000 and not 4,000,000, with neither the disks nor the leader's CPU full, and what
stops it there is not profiled yet. Durability explains little of the gap with few partitions:
with an fsync on every append Kafka still read 7.5 / 12 ms at 100 partitions, and only at 10,000
did its fsyncs, one file per partition, put it behind Queen (95 / 281 ms against 24 / 61,
[fsync runs](/benchmarks/queen-vs-kafka/#kafka-with-an-fsync-on-every-append)). The rest comes
from Queen's design: it writes about 1.7 times Kafka's bytes per message, a Queen pop is a write
too, because the lease it hands out commits through the log before the consumer sees a message,
and one leader orders every write of a raft group. On the 16-vCPU nodes Kafka went on to
4,000,000 msg/s on 100 partitions, where one Queen raft group stopped between 2,000,000 and
2,500,000.

Transactions are where that design pays. A step's acks and pushes are one log entry, so a
transaction costs about one write: 3 to 4 ms at the median. In September's comparison 9,000
single-message transactions a second went through where Kafka, Redpanda and Pulsar all fell
behind. The ceiling is the one thread that plans them: near 12,900 transactions of 10 messages a
second on the 16-vCPU nodes, and more workers do not raise it; in September Pulsar doubled its
rate with 900 partitions and 900 workers where Queen's fell.

## The rig, in short

The released 2.3.0 ran against Kafka on six GCP VMs in one zone and one VPC: three
`c4-standard-24-lssd` brokers with 24 vCPU, 88 GB and four local NVMe disks in one RAID0, and three
`c4-highcpu-16` load machines. The runs of the two days before, the transactions and the Kafka
clients used six DigitalOcean droplets with 16 vCPU and 31 GB each, in one VPC, the three with the
fastest disks as brokers: a Xeon Gold 6548N that leads Queen's raft group and two Xeon Platinum
8358. Kafka spreads its partition leaders over all three brokers. Each system ran alone on the
brokers, with the same OS settings, except Kafka's filesystem and page cache writeback on the GCP
nodes (XFS and writeback from 1 GiB, which its tail needed there).

An e2e latency is measured per message, from the instant its producer was scheduled to send it to
the instant a consumer received it, before the ack. In the 2.3.0 runs each 10-second window takes
the slowest load process, on a fresh cluster; on the DigitalOcean nodes a latency is the worst
window in seconds 30 to 60 of 90, and on the GCP nodes the median of the three windows in seconds
30 to 60 of 70, with the worst in brackets. A rate is carried when nothing was shed, nothing
erred and 99% of what was pushed was consumed; September's runs took the last 30 seconds
of 60 and called a system behind under 97%. Every number names its build and date, and the engine
moves fast: read a figure as what that build did on that rig. The GCP figures are the released
image; the DigitalOcean ones are the builds of the two days before the release. Both ran with the
binary wire forms a client turns on; the default JSON forms have not been run through the matrix.
The Jepsen campaign ran on the code of 2.3.0. The full method, each system's settings and the
commands to run it again are in [how we measure](/benchmarks/methodology/), and for the GCP runs
on [Queen vs Kafka on NVMe](/benchmarks/queen-vs-kafka/#how-we-ran-it).

Results measured on Queen 1.x, whose storage was PostgreSQL, stay in the git history of this site.
They are not 2.x numbers.

Queen replicates every write through raft, so we measure it as a cluster: three nodes, every
write fsynced on two of them before the client hears back, and a load generator that keeps its
schedule when the broker slows down, so a stall shows up as latency instead of quietly lowering
the load. Then we run the other systems on the same machines with the same load and the same
report. These pages hold what came out, each number with the conditions that make it true,
including the places where another system did better.

2.3.0 is the release these pages were measured again for. Its performance figures are from
2026-10-12 and 13, with Kafka run on the same machines; Redpanda and Pulsar were last run in
September against Queen 2.0.0-beta.2, on other machines of the same size, and those tables are
kept and labelled.

## Where Queen leads, and where it does not

Queen hardly notices the partition count. A partition costs every node about 1 KB of memory, and
its messages go into its queue's log next to every other partition's, so ten million partitions
are written and fsynced the way ten are. We are proudest of this result, because it is the reason
Queen exists: one partition per entity, as many entities as you have. Kafka gives each partition
a log and a replication state of its own. On these machines at 2,000,000 msg/s its p99 went from
25 ms at 1,000 partitions to 134 ms at 10,000, on 30 cores against Queen's 20, and at 100,000 it
fell behind while Queen read 59 ms; Queen ran the same load over ten million partitions with a
p99 of 73 ms. In September, at 1,000,000 msg/s, Pulsar and Redpanda fell behind from 50,000
partitions.

With few partitions Kafka is faster, by less than it was. At 1,000,000 msg/s on 100 partitions
Kafka delivered with an e2e of 8 / 11 ms against Queen's 10 / 19 ms, and used two thirds of
Queen's CPU; at 2,000,000 it read 8 / 43 ms against 31 / 63 ms. Kafka went on to 4,000,000 msg/s
at a p99 of 243 ms; one Queen raft group stops between 2,000,000 and 2,500,000, at the leader's
planner. Durability explains part of the gap, since Kafka answered from the page cache where Queen
waited for two fsyncs. The rest comes from two choices we made: a Queen pop is a write too,
because the lease it hands out commits through the log before the consumer sees a message, and
one leader orders every write of a raft group. Before 2.3.0 that gap was eight times at the p99
(146 ms against 18, in September).

Transactions are where that design pays. A step's acks and pushes are one log entry, so a
transaction costs about one write: 3 to 4 ms at the median. In September's comparison 9,000
single-message transactions a second went through where Kafka, Redpanda and Pulsar all fell
behind. The ceiling is the one thread that plans them: near 12,900 transactions of 10 messages a
second on today's machines, and more workers do not raise it; in September Pulsar doubled its
rate with 900 partitions and 900 workers where Queen's fell.

## The rig, in short

Six DigitalOcean droplets with 16 vCPU and 31 GB each, in one VPC: three brokers and three load
machines. For the 2.3.0 runs the three machines with the fastest disks are the brokers, a Xeon
Gold 6548N that leads Queen's raft group and two Xeon Platinum 8358; Kafka spreads its partition
leaders over all three. Each system ran alone on the brokers, with the same OS settings.

An e2e latency is measured per message, from the instant its producer was scheduled to send it to
the instant a consumer received it, before the ack. In the 2.3.0 runs a latency is the worst load
process's worst 10-second window in seconds 30 to 60 of a 90-second load on a fresh cluster, and
a rate is carried when nothing was shed, nothing erred and 99% of what was pushed was consumed;
September's runs took the last 30 seconds of 60 and called a system behind under 97%. Every number
names its build and date, and the engine moves fast: read a figure as what that build did on that
rig. The 2.3.0 figures were measured on the builds of the two days before the release, with the
binary wire forms a client turns on; the released binary and the default JSON forms have not been
run through the matrix. The Jepsen campaign ran on the code of 2.2.0. The full method, each
system's settings and the commands to run it again are in
[how we measure](/benchmarks/methodology/).

Results measured on Queen 1.x, whose storage was PostgreSQL, stay in the git history of this site.
They are not 2.x numbers.
